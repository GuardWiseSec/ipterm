Privacy policy

Last updated: October 21, 2026

ipterm has no accounts, sets no cookies and runs no analytics or tracking, and its own code stores and logs nothing about you. Cloudflare, which hosts it, keeps its own records of the traffic it carries: "Hosting and service providers" below says what they show us. This page explains the small amount of information ipterm has to handle to work.

What ipterm processes

your IP address
Every website receives your IP address when you visit it. ipterm shows it back to you. It is also used as a counter for about one minute to stop automated abuse (rate limiting), then discarded. When you look up an address or run the privacy check, a scrambled form of your IP (a one-way hash whose key changes every hour) is counted for up to an hour so one visitor can't use up the detailed location and VPN-detection data for everyone; it is kept only in memory and then forgotten. ipterm does not store your IP.
approximate location
Your city, region, network and time zone come from Cloudflare, which hosts ipterm and derives them from your IP address as part of delivering the page. ipterm displays them and does not store them.
your other IP address
A connection uses either IPv4 or IPv6, so ipterm only sees one of your addresses. To show the other one, your browser asks ipify, a free, open-source service that states it logs no visitor information, which address you have on the other protocol. ipify receives your IP address as part of that request and nothing else. ipterm does not store the result.
addresses you look up
When you look up an IP address, that address is sent to IPinfo to find its owner and location, and, unless IPinfo already recognizes it as a VPN or proxy (or a VPN provider's own list says its users reach sites from it), to proxycheck.io to check whether it is one. It is also compared with the server lists that Mullvad, Private Internet Access, IVPN and AirVPN publish, and with Apple's list of iCloud Private Relay addresses, which ipterm downloads every six hours; that comparison happens on ipterm's servers, and the address is not sent to those providers or to Apple. A public address is also sent to its regional internet registry (ARIN, RIPE NCC, APNIC, LACNIC or AFRINIC; LACNIC passes a Brazilian address on to NIC.br, Brazil's national registry) over RDAP, the same request a whois lookup makes, to find when its network was registered; if IANA's list of which registry holds which addresses can't be read, the request goes through rdap.org, which forwards it to the registry. When you look up a network (ASN), its number is sent to RIPEstat, and so is a prefix or address whose routing you look up (route, or a public range in the subnet calculator); registry (whois) lookups go to the regional internet registries. When you look up a domain, its name is sent to Cloudflare's DNS resolver (1.1.1.1), which is also used to find hostnames; a domain whois lookup sends the name to that domain's registry and registrar. Your own IP address is not passed to these services as part of a lookup, with two exceptions: when you open the homepage or run whoami, Cloudflare's resolver is asked for your address's hostname (reverse DNS), and the privacy check described below sends it to IPinfo and proxycheck.io. Your own address is never sent to a registry for its network's registration date, even when you look it up by number (only a whois lookup of it goes to its registry). ipterm stores none of it.
mail checks
When you run a mail check (mail, or a /mail/ page), the domain name is sent to Cloudflare's DNS resolver (1.1.1.1) to read its mail records (MX, SPF, DMARC, DKIM, MTA-STS, TLS-RPT and BIMI). If the domain publishes an MTA-STS record, ipterm also fetches its policy file from https://mta-sts.<domain>, a server run by whoever runs that domain. That request comes from ipterm's servers at Cloudflare, not from your browser, so it carries neither your IP address nor anything else about you. The result is cached for five minutes, keyed by the domain, with no record of who asked.
domain report cards and badges
When you run a report card (grade, or a /grade/ page), ipterm reads the domain's DNS records through Cloudflare's resolver, runs the mail check described above, reads its registration from the registry (as a whois lookup does), and asks the domain's own website for its headers: a HEAD request to https://<domain>/ and one to http://<domain>/, following redirects only within that domain, at most five requests in all. A HEAD request asks for headers only. If the site refuses it (HTTP 405 or 501), ipterm asks once more with a GET and drops the page unread as soon as the headers arrive. The requests identify themselves as ipterm (ipterm-grade in the User-Agent). Like the mail check's, these requests come from ipterm's servers at Cloudflare, not from your browser, so the website sees neither your IP address nor anything else about you. The report card is cached for five minutes, keyed by the domain, with no record of who asked. A README badge made with the /badge/<domain>.json address is drawn by shields.io, which asks ipterm for the grade, so people who view the README never contact ipterm. The /badge/<domain>.svg image is served by ipterm itself: where it is shown without a proxy in between, each viewer's browser fetches it from ipterm, which sees their IP address as part of that request, as any website does, and stores nothing about them. Both badges are cached for an hour, keyed by the domain.
privacy check (leaktest)
Only if you run the leaktest command (or dnsleak) or open the leak test page: your IP address is compared with those VPN providers' server lists and Apple's iCloud Private Relay list, and looked up with IPinfo and, unless ipterm's copy of those lists names it, proxycheck.io, to see whether it is recognized as a VPN, proxy or data-center address (and which VPN service, if known), and your address on the other IP version, if you have one, with IPinfo alone, for its network (neither goes to a registry), your browser contacts a STUN server run by Cloudflare (stun.cloudflare.com) to see which public addresses WebRTC reveals, your device's time zone and your browser's language setting are compared with your IP's location inside your browser, and your browser reads the summary Cloudflare's edge gives of its own connection to ipterm (the TLS and HTTP versions, the key exchange, and whether the connection comes through Cloudflare WARP). On ipterm's server, your address is also compared with the Tor Project's public list of Tor exit addresses, which ipterm downloads about once an hour; your address is never sent to the Tor Project. The check's results are shown to you and not saved. Your last result is kept in the open page's memory, so that running the check again (say, after turning a VPN on) can show what changed; it is never written to your device's storage, and it's gone when you close or reload the page. As with any lookup, IPinfo's answer for an address is cached for up to six hours, and proxycheck.io's for up to one hour, so repeat lookups are fast; those cache entries contain only the address and its network details, nothing about you or your browser. ipterm asks proxycheck.io not to keep these queries in its log.
DNS leak test
Part of the privacy check: your browser looks up a few random, single-use names such as 3f9a0c41d27be5806a11.dl.ipterm.net, and your device's DNS resolver (usually run by your internet provider, your VPN or a public DNS service such as Cloudflare's or Google's) asks ipterm's own DNS server about them. That server runs on a small virtual server ipterm rents from a hosting company, outside Cloudflare. It notes the IP address of the resolver that asked, next to the random name, keeps it only in memory and for less than two minutes, and deletes it as soon as ipterm's site at Cloudflare collects it for your check. It keeps no log of names or addresses. It sees your resolver's address rather than yours, unless you run your own resolver at home, and it ignores the part of your address that some resolvers attach to their queries (EDNS Client Subnet). The resolvers' public addresses are looked up with IPinfo to name their networks, and shown to you; addresses from private networks are shown but not looked up. The random names identify nothing but that one check, and nothing is saved.
shared leak test results
After the privacy check, the share command copies a link such as ipterm.net/leaktest/r/1bjp. Your browser makes it, and it holds only the grade, whether a VPN was detected, and which checks passed, leaked or weren't tested: no IP address, provider, location or DNS resolver. Everyone with the same result gets the same link, so a link can't be traced back to you. Opening someone's link shows their result and never runs the check on your browser; that only happens if you choose "run yours". ipterm keeps no record of links made or opened.
port check
Only if you run the port command (or ask for /port/<number> from a command-line tool): ipterm's server opens one connection to your own IP address, the one your request came from, on the port you name, to see whether anything answers, and closes it at once without sending anything. It never connects to any other address, and the result is shown to you and not saved. Pages that explain a port never run the check by themselves. Your address counts toward a separate limit of 10 checks a minute, kept for about a minute like the one above.
cached results
Lookup results are cached for up to six hours, and the registry record of an address or network (a whois lookup, or the registration date a lookup shows) for a day, so repeated lookups are fast. The cache is keyed by the address that was looked up, with no record of who asked (if you look up or check your own address, that entry is for your address).
browser storage
If you change the color theme or turn off the CRT effects, your choice is saved in your own browser (local storage). The commands you type in the terminal are saved there too (the newest 200, never their results), so the arrow keys, history and Tab completion still know them after a reload. history clear deletes them and history off stops saving them. None of it leaves your device, and you can clear it at any time.

Hosting and service providers

ipterm runs on Cloudflare, which handles every request to deliver the site, under its own privacy policy. ipterm's own code stores and logs nothing about you, but Cloudflare keeps its own records of the traffic it carries. Its analytics show us the site's traffic, mostly in aggregate (which countries requests come from, which pages are asked for, which browsers and tools ask), and a sample of individual requests with their IP address, country, page and user agent, whether or not a firewall or bot rule acted on them, and we can see up to the last 31 days of them. Every lookup has its own URL (such as /8.8.8.8), so these records can include what was looked up, and the privacy check's lookup of your other IP version puts that address in its URL. Browsers may also send Cloudflare a short report when a page fails to load (Network Error Logging; Cloudflare adds the headers that ask for it). Lookups use IPinfo, proxycheck.io, RIPEstat and the regional internet registries' RDAP services as described above, and ipify shows your other IP address. The DNS leak test's DNS server runs on a virtual server rented from a hosting company, as described above, and keeps no logs. The list of Tor exit addresses comes from the Tor Project, the VPN server lists from Mullvad, Private Internet Access, IVPN and AirVPN, and the iCloud Private Relay list from Apple, all downloaded by ipterm's servers at Cloudflare without anything about you. The site's fonts and map data are served from ipterm itself, so no font or map service sees your visit.

Sponsored links

ipterm may show one short line marked sponsored next to some results. It is plain text and a link: no ad scripts, no tracking pixels and no cookies, and nothing about you is shared with sponsors. Some sponsored links include a referral code that identifies ipterm and the part of the site that showed the link (for example, the privacy check), so the sponsor can credit the visit. The code is the same for everyone who sees that link: it identifies the placement, never you. Once you click through, the sponsor's own privacy policy applies.

Your choices and rights

Apart from Cloudflare's records described above, ipterm does not keep personal information tied to you, so there is usually nothing to access, export or delete. If you have a question or a request under privacy laws such as the GDPR or CCPA, contact us using the address below and we will respond.

Children

ipterm is a general technical tool and is not directed at children under 13.

Changes

If ipterm starts handling information differently, for example by adding analytics, this page will be updated first and the date at the top will change.
