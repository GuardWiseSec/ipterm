What your IP address reveals (and what it doesn’t)

Updated October 28, 2026 · 2 min read

Every website you visit sees your public IP address, because that is where it sends its reply. On its own, the address says less than many people fear, and more than some people assume.

What an IP address does reveal

  • Your network. Every public address belongs to an organization: usually your internet provider, but sometimes a mobile carrier, university, company, cloud host or VPN service. Its ASN identifies it.
  • An approximate location. Usually a city or region, sometimes only a country. It often points to your provider's regional hub, which can be tens or even hundreds of kilometers from where you are.
  • A guess at your time zone, based on that location.
  • Whether it's a datacenter or VPN address, to services that keep lists of those.

What it does not reveal

  • Your name, email address or phone number.
  • Your street address or precise location.
  • Your browsing history or which devices are on your network.

Who can connect an IP address to you

Your internet provider knows which customer had which address at a given time. Providers generally disclose that only through a legal process, such as a court order or subpoena, and the rules differ by country. Websites themselves can link visits to you only if you also sign in or share identifying details.

Why your address changes, or is shared

  • Dynamic addresses: most home connections get an address that can change from time to time, for example when the router restarts.
  • Carrier-grade NAT: many mobile and some home providers put many customers behind one public address, so the address points to the provider more than to you.

Showing websites a different address

A VPN routes your traffic through the VPN provider, so websites see the provider's address instead of yours. Your internet provider then sees only that you're connected to the VPN, while the VPN provider can see where your traffic goes, so choose one you trust. Tor spreads traffic across volunteer relays for stronger anonymity at the cost of speed. Neither changes what you reveal by signing in to accounts.

Who looks up the sites you visit

Before your device connects to a site, a DNS resolver looks up the site's address, so the resolver sees the names of the sites you visit. Devices use their internet provider's resolver unless they, the router or a VPN are set to use another, such as one of the public DNS resolvers. leaktest shows which one your browser actually uses.
